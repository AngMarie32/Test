The Active Trader Tax Playbook

Five rules that move the number on an active trader's return. Read the one that matches how you trade, then book a call if you want us to check your numbers.

1. Trader Tax Status (TTS) self-check

There is no bright-line test in the tax code. Courts have looked at whether your activity is substantial, frequent, regular and continuous, and whether you seek profit from short-term price swings. Check how many of these fit you:

  • Hundreds of trades a year - court cases often discuss roughly 700+ round trips
  • You trade on most available market days (think 75%+)
  • Your average holding period is short - days or weeks, not months
  • You spend a large part of your working day trading or researching
  • You have a dedicated setup: data feeds, platforms, a trading log

Why it matters: with TTS, trading expenses (data, platforms, education, equipment, home office) can be deducted as business expenses on Schedule C. Without TTS, those are investment expenses that most individual traders currently cannot deduct at all.

2. Section 475(f) mark-to-market election

Traders who qualify for TTS can elect mark-to-market (MTM) on securities. Effects:

  • Gains and losses become ordinary - the $3,000 net capital loss limit no longer applies to those trades
  • The wash sale rule does not apply to MTM securities
  • Open positions are treated as sold at fair value on Dec 31
  • Trade-off: no long-term capital gain rates on MTM securities, and the election is hard to revoke

Deadline: existing individual traders generally must elect by the original due date of the prior year's return (usually April 15) by attaching a statement to that return or extension, then file Form 3115 with the return for the year of the election. A new entity can elect internally within 2 months and 15 days of the start of its first tax year. Miss it and you wait a year.

3. Futures and Section 1256 contracts: the 60/40 rule

Regulated futures, broad-based index options (like SPX) and certain other contracts are taxed 60% long-term / 40% short-term regardless of holding period, and are marked to market at year-end. They are reported on Form 6781.

Illustrative example: $100,000 net gain, top bracketApprox. top federal rate
Short-term stock trades37%
Section 1256 futures (60% × 20% + 40% × 37%)26.8%

Excludes the 3.8% net investment income tax and state tax. Your bracket determines your actual rates.

Net 1256 losses can also be carried back up to three years against prior 1256 gains with an election on Form 6781.

4. Prop firm / funded trader payouts

  • Payouts are usually reported on Form 1099-NEC or 1099-MISC - they are generally compensation for services, not capital gains
  • That typically means Schedule C income plus self-employment tax (15.3% up to the Social Security wage base, 2.9% Medicare above it)
  • Evaluation fees, resets, data and platform subscriptions are generally deductible against that income
  • Plan quarterly estimated payments - payouts have no withholding

5. Year-end wash sale audit

A loss is disallowed if you buy substantially identical securities within 30 days before or after the sale - across all your accounts, including IRAs (where the loss can be lost permanently). Before Dec 31:

  • List every position closed at a loss in December
  • Check re-entries in the same or substantially identical security - in every account and your spouse's
  • Remember January re-entries within 30 days still trigger the rule
  • Brokers only track wash sales within one account - reconcile across brokers yourself

Quarterly estimates for lumpy P&L

Safe harbor: pay 100% of last year's tax (110% if last year's AGI was over $150,000) or 90% of this year's. Traders whose profits come late in the year can often use the annualized income installment method (Form 2210, Schedule AI) to avoid overpaying in early quarters.
